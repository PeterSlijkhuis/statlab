The grid of lesson 15.1 works for any prior, but for a proportion there is a
shortcut. When the prior is a beta distribution and the data are counts of
successes, the posterior is a beta distribution too, and you can write it down
without a grid.

## The beta shortcut

A `Beta(a, b)` prior behaves like `a` earlier successes and `b` earlier failures.
Add the new data and the posterior is `Beta(a + successes, b + failures)`. The
flat prior of the last lesson is `Beta(1, 1)`.

<CodeBlock id="c-conjugate" code={`d <- read.csv("data/workplace.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)

left_all <- sum(d$left_company)
stayed_all <- nrow(d) - left_all

p_grid <- seq(0, 1, length.out = 1001)
grid_post <- dbinom(left_all, size = nrow(d), prob = p_grid)
grid_post <- grid_post / sum(grid_post)

# The same posterior from the shortcut, scaled to the grid for comparison.
beta_post <- dbeta(p_grid, 1 + left_all, 1 + stayed_all)
beta_post <- beta_post / sum(beta_post)

max(abs(grid_post - beta_post))`} />

The two agree to many decimal places. From here on, `qbeta()`, `pbeta()` and
`rbeta()` answer questions about the posterior directly.

## The credible interval

The middle 95 % of the posterior is a **95 % credible interval**. `qbeta()` gives
the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

<CodeBlock id="c-credible" code={`credible <- qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), 1 + left_all, 1 + stayed_all)
round(credible, 3)

# The frequentist 95 % confidence interval, for comparison.
round(binom.test(left_all, nrow(d))$conf.int, 3)`} />

The numbers are almost the same, 0.192 to 0.267 against 0.190 to 0.267. With a
flat prior and plenty of data they usually are. The **meaning** is different,
and the difference is the reason some researchers prefer the Bayesian version:

- The **credible interval** says: given these data and this prior, there is a
  95 % probability that the leaving rate lies between 19.2 % and 26.7 %.
- The **confidence interval** says: this interval came from a procedure that
  captures the true rate in 95 % of samples. Lesson 7.2 showed that you cannot
  put a probability on this one interval.

The sentence people want to say about a confidence interval is the one a credible
interval actually licenses.

## The probability of a claim

A posterior answers questions a p-value cannot. HR's target is a leaving rate
below 20 %. How probable is it that the company is above target?

<CodeBlock id="c-claim" code={`# The share of the posterior above 0.20.
pbeta(0.20, 1 + left_all, 1 + stayed_all, lower.tail = FALSE)

# And above 25 %?
pbeta(0.25, 1 + left_all, 1 + stayed_all, lower.tail = FALSE)`} />

About a 93 % probability that the rate is above 20 %, and about 13 % that it is
above 25 %. Those are statements a manager can act on.

## Comparing two groups by simulation

For two groups, draw thousands of plausible rates from each posterior with
`rbeta()`, and compare them draw by draw. Each pair of draws is one plausible
state of the world, so the share of pairs in which one rate is lower is the
probability that it is lower.

<CodeBlock id="c-simulate" code={`left_trained <- sum(d$left_company[d$training == "Yes"])
n_trained <- sum(d$training == "Yes")
left_untrained <- sum(d$left_company[d$training == "No"])
n_untrained <- sum(d$training == "No")

set.seed(1502)
rate_trained <- rbeta(10000, 1 + left_trained, 1 + n_trained - left_trained)
rate_untrained <- rbeta(10000, 1 + left_untrained, 1 + n_untrained - left_untrained)

# Probability that training goes with a lower leaving rate.
mean(rate_trained < rate_untrained)

# A 95 % credible interval for the difference in rates.
round(quantile(rate_trained - rate_untrained, c(0.025, 0.975)), 3)`} />

A probability close to one half, and an interval for the difference that runs
about 7 points either way. The data do not tell the two groups apart. Simulation takes the place of a formula here, and it works for any
quantity you can compute from the draws: a ratio, a difference, whether both
rates are above 20 %.
